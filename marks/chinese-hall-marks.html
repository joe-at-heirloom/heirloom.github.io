Chinese hall marks

Instead of a reign, some Chinese porcelain is marked with the name of a hall, a studio or a palace building: 慎德堂製, “made for the Hall of Cautious Virtue.” Here are 125 of them, whose each was, and the wares they appear on.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A hall mark names the hall or studio an item was made for, or claims to be. Emperors, empresses, princes, officials and scholars all had them, and so did Republic-period presidents and Jingdezhen painters. Like reign marks, they were copied, so a hall mark names what it claims and does not by itself date the item. Most hall marks end in 堂 (hall), 齋 (studio), 宮 or 殿 (palace hall), 書屋 or 山房 (study); the last character, 製, means “made.”

中和堂製

Zhonghe tang zhi; Kangxi xinhai / renzi / guichou Zhonghe tang zhi

Made for (or at) the Hall of Central Harmony; dated variants add the Kangxi reign name and a cyclical year.

Whose: Disputed: early-Kangxi imperial kiln (Geng Baochang) vs. a pavilion built in 1671 by the official Fan Wenying (范文英, zi 瞻斗) in the Jinhua prefectural yamen, Zhejiang (Lam 2024). A Yuanmingyuan attribution circulates but is rejected on dating grounds.1, 2, 3

On wares of: Kangxi xinhai (1671); Kangxi renzi (1672); Kangxi guichou (1673)2, 3, 4, 5, 6

How it is written: Eight characters in four columns for the dated form (Li Suheng (2022)); script not stated in sources read.2, 3, 4

Found on: Dishes (lotus-form shou dish), bowls, small dishes with poems and a 'Zhangong' seal, paper-thin cups imitating Chenghua chicken cups, coffee-glazed bowls (Percival David).3, 4, 5

慎德堂製

Shèndé Táng zhì

Made for the Hall of Prudent Virtue

Whose: Daoguang Emperor; his residence in the Yuanmingyuan (Jiuzhou Qingyan area), Hall completed 1831 (道光十一年); Daoguang died there 1850; destroyed 1860 (date of destruction disputed, see disagreements)32, 33

On wares of: Daoguang (1831–1850); Guangxu copies (1875–1908); Republic copies (1912–1949)33, 34, 35, 36

How it is written: Regular script (楷书), written with a slanted 'side-brush' stroke; four characters in two columns; a rarer three-character vertical 慎德堂 form Iron red (矾红/红彩) on the base (and inside the cover knob of covered bowls); gold on red-ground items where red could not be used32, 34, 37, 38, 39

Found on: Famille rose (white, yellow, green, purple grounds), some doucai and blue and white; monochromes (red, yellow, green, sauce, blue); bowls, covered bowls, dishes, spoons, zhadou, bird feeders, vases, jars, flowerpots38 (one source)

Later copies: Guangxu copies (vases, zun) lack the side-brush stroke and often add a red double square; Republic copies also lack it. Seal-script or underglaze-blue 慎德堂 marks are considered later copies by the Palace Museum Journal (2010).34, 36

Before you rely on a hall mark

  • A hall mark names the hall, studio or palace building a item was made for, or claims to be. Hall marks were copied too (慎德堂製 under Guangxu and in the Republic; 居仁堂製 after 1916), so the mark alone does not date the item or show that it is genuine.107
  • Many hall names were used by more than one owner, and many private hall marks cannot be tied to a person: the same characters can belong to different households in different centuries.107

Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine or made for the hall its mark names. A specialist who can handle it is the one to ask. For reign marks, see Chinese reign marks.
