Nippon marks

“Nippon” on the base of a vase or a plate is not a maker's name. It is the Japanese name for Japan, and it dates an item to a span of about thirty years.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A US law of 1890 required imports, from March 1, 1891, to name their country of origin in English 1, 2, and Japanese exporters marked their wares “Nippon.” In 1921 the US Treasury ruled that Nippon was a Japanese word, not an English one, and required “Japan” instead 2, 3, 4. So a Nippon backstamp used as the country name points to 1891 to 1921 2, 5.

What Nippon means, and who used it

Section 6 of the Tariff Act of 1890, the McKinley Tariff, said that from March 1, 1891, imported goods of a kind usually marked had to be marked in legible English words with their country of origin, or they would not be admitted 1, 2. Japanese exporters met the rule with the word Nippon 2, 5.

In 1921 the Treasury decided that Nippon was the Japanese name for Japan, that the English word was Japan, and that goods marked only Nippon no longer met the law 2, 3, 4. After that, Japanese wares for the US were marked Japan or Made in Japan 2, 5, 8.

Nippon was not one factory. Many Japanese makers, decorators and exporters used it, often with a symbol or name of their own 2, 9. The best known is the Morimura group: Morimura Bros. of New York and, from 1904, its factory company Nippon Toki, the maker now called Noritake 2, 4, 10. The rule was a US one, so wares made for other markets often do not say Nippon 5, 6.

Nippon marks, in order

Where we have no open-license photograph or period-book facsimile of a mark, its card describes it in words. Most are printed under the glaze or over it on the base, in green, blue, magenta, red or gold 2, 3.

Nippon in the backstamp

1891 to 1921

Nippon alone, or with Hand Painted and a maker's symbol. As the country name, it dates the item to between the McKinley Tariff and the 1921 ruling 1, 2, 5. It does not name the maker 2, 9.

The maple leaf

From the 1890s or the 1900s to 1921

A small leaf above the word Nippon, linked to the Morimura group 2, 6. Van Patten dates it from 1891; Murphy says it was registered in Japan in 1908, though items with it seem earlier 2, 6. On the genuine mark the leaf is about a quarter of an inch across 2, 7.

The M in a wreath

About 1911 to 1921 with Nippon

A capital M, for Morimura, inside a leafy wreath that is open at the top, with Hand Painted above and Nippon below 2, 4. Van Patten dates it from 1911; Alden and Richardson date their first Morimura backstamp to 1891, registered in Japan in 1911 2, 4. The same wreath continued after 1921 with Noritake and Made in Japan 2, 4.

The RC mark

About 1906 or 1911 to 1921

The letters RC on a balancing-toy device, with Hand Painted and Nippon, used by Nippon Toki, usually on its better wares 2, 4. Van Patten gives 1906 for its first use and 1911 for its registration in Japan and the US 2. Alden and Richardson read RC as Royal Ceramic; others read Royal Crockery 2, 4.

The komaru

About 1908 or 1912 to 1921

A round emblem with Hand Painted Nippon, a Nippon Toki mark; Murphy says it was first registered in 1908, Van Patten gives 1912 2, 5, 6.

The rising sun

About 1911 to 1921

A half sun with rays, with Nippon or Hand Painted, printed in blue 2, 7. The sources seen do not name its maker.

Japan in place of Nippon

From 1921

Japan or Made in Japan where Nippon would have been means 1921 or later 2, 4, 5, 8. During the occupation, from 1945 to 1952, some wares for the US were marked Occupied Japan, though Japan and Made in Japan were used then too 2, 11.

Fake Nippon marks to be careful with

  • Since the 1980s new porcelain has been made with backstamps copied from or modeled on Nippon marks, mainly in East Asia 2, 7. The reference books show these fake marks beside the genuine ones.
  • Fakes the books describe: a wreath turned upside down with an hourglass or a K inside; a wreath closed at the top; a maple leaf about half an inch wide; a rising sun drawn as a hollow outline or with a gap between the sun and its rays; an RC mark printed all in green 2, 7.
  • These items can be imported legally: US law requires the real country of origin in English, and a decorative “Nippon” mark is allowed beside it. The real origin is often on a paper label that comes off, while the copied mark is fired on 2, 7.
  • Some genuine Nippon-era items have no backstamp at all; in a set, a saucer may be marked when its cup is not 2, 3.
  • We found no US Customs notice, museum statement or newspaper report on fake Nippon marks; the reference books above are the record.

Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine. A mark says what it claims; a specialist who can handle the item is the one to ask.

Where the sources disagree

  • When the 1921 change took effect: Van Patten reprints the Treasury notice as dated March 1, 1921, taking effect six months later, about September 1 2; Baker gives “after March 1, 1921” 12; most sources say only 1921.
  • Start dates of the maple leaf, M-in-wreath, RC and komaru marks differ by source, as given under each mark above.

Questions

What does Nippon on the bottom of porcelain mean?

It is the Japanese name for Japan. US law from 1891 required imports to name their country of origin, and Japanese exporters wrote Nippon until 1921 1, 2, 5. It tells you where and roughly when, not who.

Why did Nippon change to Japan in 1921?

The US Treasury ruled that Nippon was a Japanese word and that the law required the English name, Japan 2, 3, 4.

Was Nippon porcelain made by Noritake?

Some was. The Morimura group, whose factory company became Noritake, used the maple leaf, the M in a wreath, RC and the komaru; many other firms used Nippon too 2, 4, 6.

Can an item marked Nippon be new?

Yes. Reference books have documented new porcelain with copied Nippon marks since the 1980s 2, 7. Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine.
