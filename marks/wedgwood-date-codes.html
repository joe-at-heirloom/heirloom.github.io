Wedgwood date codes, 1860 to 1930

Wedgwood earthenware from 1860 to 1930 carries three impressed characters beside the WEDGWOOD mark. Type them to get the year and month.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A Wedgwood date code is three impressed characters next to the impressed WEDGWOOD mark. Only the last character is the year: in TOT, the final T is 1865 or 1891. ENGLAND in the mark (from 1891) usually decides between the two cycles.

Decode a Wedgwood date code

Type the three impressed characters next to WEDGWOOD, in order.

How the code works

The impressed WEDGWOOD mark on a cream-colored platter that the Met dates to about 1780, before the date codes began. On wares from 1860 to 1930 the three-letter code is impressed in the same way, in a row beside or below this word. No openly licensed photograph of a dated code has been found yet, so the letters below are set in type. Photograph: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Open Access (public domain), via Wikimedia Commons (platter, SF2014.712.9), cropped by Heirloom.

The code is three impressed characters in a row beside the impressed WEDGWOOD mark. The first is the month (from 1907, the figure 3; from 1924, 4), the second identifies the potter, and only the last is the year.

Read these before you rely on a year

  • A date code only means something next to the right factory mark (impressed WEDGWOOD, impressed MINTON(S), the Royal Worcester printed mark, the Rookwood RP monogram, the Sevres interlaced Ls). A code alone does not identify the maker, and several of these systems were copied or forged (Minton by smaller makers; Sevres very widely).
  • The codes give a single year only when read exactly. Letters that look alike (I/J, U/V, O/Q, C/G) and blurred or glaze-filled impressions are the main source of error. When a reading is uncertain, report the candidate years.
  • The date code is three impressed characters in a row, next to the impressed WEDGWOOD mark. Only the LAST character is the year. A single letter or scattered letters are potters' or tally marks, not dates (W2, W4).
  • First character, 1860 to 1906, is the month (W1, W4): J=January, F=February, A=April, S=September, O=October, N=November, D=December, T=June, W=August. For 1860 to 1863: M=March, Y=May, V=July. From 1864: R=March, M=May, L=July. So 'M' means March before 1864 and May from 1864.
  • Because 1864 changed the month letters, a first letter Y or V (May or July, old style) points to 1860 to 1863, and a first letter R or L points to 1864 or later. This is a rule stated in W1; apply it only when the code is clearly read.
  • Second character: a letter identifying the potter or workman (W1, W4). It carries no date information.
  • From 1907 the first character is the figure 3 instead of a month letter. From 1924 it is 4 (W1 table, W2). W4 says 3 continued through 1924 and 4 began in 1925. So a code '3?A' or '4?A' (A = 1924) is the one place where the prefix sources disagree; the year letter A = 1924 is not in dispute.
  • Cycles 1 and 2 reuse the same letters (O to Z, then A to I/N), so a code like 'TOT' could be June 1865 or June 1891 (W1). Tie-breakers: the word ENGLAND is added to the mark from 1891 (W1, W2, W3); MADE IN ENGLAND appears on some wares from 1898 and generally from about 1908 (W1). Items with no ENGLAND are normally from cycle 1 or from 1886 to 1890. W1 and W2 both warn that small items and items not made for export sometimes lack ENGLAND or MADE IN ENGLAND, so absence alone is not proof.
  • The flag england_expected in each row is taken from the sources' ENGLAND rule (1891+). It is a guide, not proof.
  • End of the letter system: W4 says it ended with F = 1929. W2 lists G with a 4 prefix as 1930. W1's chart says 'after 1930' the month is given as a number and the year as two digits (e.g. '1A 32' = January 1932, A being the workman). W1's text says the change came in 1929 ('30 standing for 1930'). W2 says two- or four-digit years appear from about 1935, not always before about 1950. The row G / 1930 therefore rests on W2 alone.
  • After the letter system: codes like '1A32' mean month number (1 = January), workman letter, then the last two digits of the year (1932) (W1). Do not read these with the letter table.
  • W2 says the three-letter code is rarely found on jasper or basalt. Its absence tells you nothing about date.
  • Marks with '& CO' (Wedgwood & Co) or the spelling WEDGEWOOD (William Smith & Co) are not Josiah Wedgwood & Sons, and this table does not apply to them (W1).
  • Bone china has separate conventions. W4's replies say the three-letter code belongs to earthenware (queensware, majolica, pearlware). Treat a three-letter reading on bone china with caution.

Questions

How do I date Wedgwood by its marks?

Look for the impressed WEDGWOOD mark and a group of three impressed characters beside it. The last character is the year letter; the first gives the month or, from 1907, the period. ENGLAND in the mark means 1891 or later.

Why does my code give two years?

The year letters were reused: O to Z ran in 1860–1871 and again in 1886–1897. ENGLAND on the base (from 1891) usually decides it, though small items and wares not made for export sometimes lack it.

What about a code like 1A32?

That is the later system: month number, potter's letter, then the last two digits of the year (January 1932). The letter table does not apply.
