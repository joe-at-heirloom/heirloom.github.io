Meissen marks

Meissen's crossed swords are among the most copied marks in porcelain. The small changes to the swords over three centuries narrow the date, though only roughly.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

Meissen, near Dresden, was Europe's first maker of hard-paste porcelain, founded by Augustus the Strong in 1710 3, 5, 7. Its blue crossed swords, from the 1720s, are still used 1, 5, 7. A dot between the hilts points to about 1763 to 1774, and a star between them to the Marcolini years, 1774 to 1814 1, 3, 8. Every source warns that the mark alone does not date an item 1, 2.

Who made Meissen porcelain

Augustus II, Elector of Saxony and King of Poland, founded the factory in 1710, with Johann Friedrich Böttger as its key figure; it was the first in Europe to make hard-paste porcelain 3, 5, 7. It worked in the Albrechtsburg castle at Meissen, near Dresden 5, 7. Böttger made a red stoneware first, from about 1707, and died in 1719 3, 5.

Johann Gregorius Höroldt came in 1720 and led the painters for some twenty years; Johann Joachim Kändler came in 1731 and became chief modeler in 1733 3, 5. Count Camillo Marcolini directed the factory from 1774 to 1814 1, 3, 5.

In the 1860s the works moved out of the castle to a new factory in the Triebisch valley 5, 7. The factory has never closed and still makes porcelain under the crossed swords 5, 7.

Meissen porcelain is often called Dresden china, after the nearby city, though Dresden was also home to many other decorators and makers 2, 3.

Meissen marks, in order

Almost all of these are painted in blue under the glaze 1, 3. Dates are approximate, and the sources disagree on several of them; where they do, each source's dates are given.

The AR monogram

About 1710 to 1740, by different accounts

AR, for Augustus Rex, in interlaced letters. It marked porcelain for the king's own use or as royal gifts 1, 3. Berling dates it about 1725 to 1740, Chaffers about 1710 to 1712, and the Meissen town museum 1710 to 1725 1, 3, 8. It was later copied widely; see below.

K.P.M. and similar letters

1723 to about 1730

K.P.M. (Königliche Porzellan-Manufaktur), and the variants M.P.M. and K.P.F., sometimes with small crossed swords: the factory's first mark against outside decorators, announced in 1723 1, 3, 7. It is not the K.P.M. of the Berlin factory, whose mark adds a scepter or orb 1, 3.

The caduceus

Between about 1712 and 1735, by different accounts

A staff with a single snake wound round it, Mercury's staff, used on ware for trade and export 1, 3, 4. Berling dates it 1727 to 1735; Chaffers and Hooper 1712 to 1720; the town museum 1720 to 1730 1, 3, 4, 8.

The crossed swords

From the 1720s to today

Two crossed swords from the arms of the Elector of Saxony, in blue under the glaze. When they were first used is disputed: Meissen gives 1722, Berling says not before 1725 and in general use by about 1730, and the German patent office says generally from 1731 1, 5, 7. Early swords often have long blades and curved guards with knob ends 1, 3. On figures the swords are often small and placed low, where they show above a mount 1, 3.

Swords with court initials

About the 1720s to the 1760s

Initials of the Saxon and Polish court kitchens beside the swords, such as K.H.C. for the Königliche Hof-Conditorei, usually in purple over the glaze, on porcelain made for the court services 1, 3.

A dot between the hilts

About 1763 to 1774

A dot between the handles of the swords, called the dot or King's period 3, 8. Chaffers and the town museum date it 1763 to 1774; Berling and Doenges about 1756 to 1780 1, 2, 3, 8. Doenges notes that dots also occur as workers' marks outside this period 2.

A star between the hilts

About 1774 to 1814

A small star between the handles, sometimes with a numeral such as 4 below: the Marcolini period 1, 2, 3, 8. Chaffers gives 1774 to 1814; Berling and Doenges from about 1780 to just after 1814 1, 2, 3.

Swords incised in a triangle

The later 1700s

On unglazed biscuit porcelain the swords were incised inside a triangle, with a star under them in the Marcolini period 1, 2, 3.

Swords with numerals, and later forms

After 1814

Just after 1814 the swords were painted with Roman or Arabic numerals 1, 2, 8. The town museum's display dates swords with knobbed pommels 1860 to 1924, and swords with a dot between the blade tips, from the directorship of Max Adolf Pfeiffer, 1924 to 1933 8. The German patent office says year signs have been impressed in the base since 1948 7.

Cuts through the swords

From about 1760

A line or lines ground through the glaze across or beside the swords. They mark ware the factory sold as seconds or sold undecorated: Berling and Doenges describe a cut across the swords on porcelain sold white, so any painting on such an item was added outside the factory 1, 2. Meissen says today that one grind mark below the swords means second choice and several mean faulty goods 2, 6.

Look-alike and copied marks to be careful with

  • Outside decorators painted Meissen white ware and sold it as factory work from the 1720s; the K.P.M. mark and the cuts through the swords were the factory's answers 1, 2.
  • Other German factories drew marks to look like the swords: a crossed hayfork at Rudolstadt, a crossed W at Wallendorf, crossed L's at Limbach, among others 1, 2, 3.
  • Dresden marks. Many firms in Dresden decorated or made porcelain in the Meissen style, and some printed Dresden marks of their own; a Dresden mark is not a Meissen mark 2, 3.
  • Copies abroad. French, English and German firms copied Meissen models and marks; Doenges wrote in 1907 that about half the “old Meissen” in the trade was not genuine 2, 3, 9.
  • The AR monogram was copied on later porcelain decorated in Dresden; Chaffers printed the original and the later AR side by side 3. Samson of Paris, Chaffers says, put an S between crossed swords on its copies 3.

Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine. A mark says what it claims; a specialist who can handle the item is the one to ask.

Where the sources disagree

  • The founding year: 1710 3, 5, or 1709 in Hooper 4.
  • The dates of the AR, caduceus, dot and star marks, and the first use of the swords, as given under each mark above 1, 2, 3, 7, 8.

Questions

How do I date Meissen by the swords?

Only roughly. A dot between the hilts points to about 1763 to 1774 and a star to 1774 to 1814; other forms need the style, paste and glaze as well 1, 2, 3.

What does a line through the Meissen swords mean?

The factory ground lines through or beside the swords to mark seconds or ware sold undecorated 1, 2, 6.

Is K.P.M. Meissen?

Early Meissen used K.P.M. from 1723 to about 1730 1, 7. K.P.M. with a scepter or orb is the Berlin factory's mark 1, 3.

Is Dresden porcelain the same as Meissen?

Not always. Meissen is near Dresden and is often called Dresden china, but many other Dresden firms decorated and made porcelain with their own marks 2, 3.

Does a crossed-swords mark mean my porcelain is Meissen?

No. It is among the most copied marks in porcelain 2, 3. Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine.
