German silver and nickel silver

German silver looks like silver and has none in it. Here is what it is, its other names, and the marks that tell you.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

German silver, also called nickel silver, is an alloy of copper, nickel and zinc with no silver in it. It is marked with one of its names, such as NICKEL SILVER or ALP, or EPNS when it has been electroplated with a thin coat of silver. It is not the same as solid silver made in Germany, which carries a number such as 800.

German silver has no silver in it. It is an alloy of copper, nickel and zinc, chosen because its color is close to silver's and it takes a fine polish. It was used on its own and, more often, as the base metal under silver plate.

What it is made of

The sources agree on the three metals but give different proportions, because makers used many recipes:

  • The US Bureau of Standards (1921): copper 52 to 80%, zinc 10 to 35%, nickel 5 to 30%, with high grades at about 20% nickel and low grades under 10%.
  • Meyers (1908): in effect brass with 16 to 33% nickel added, usually 25%.
  • The Britannica of 1911: for imitation silver knives and forks, about half copper and the rest zinc and nickel in about equal parts.

All three name a Chinese alloy, paktong (packfong), as the same kind of metal, known long before the European names.

Its names

The same alloy is sold under many names. German silver and nickel silver are the usual English ones; the Bureau of Standards noted in 1921 that the American trade had moved to "nickel silver" after the war. Neusilber and Argentan are German names, maillechort the French one, and both lists add others such as China silver and new silver. Alpacca (Alpaka) is a name both for the alloy and, in Meyers, for silver-plated nickel silver: the sources differ, so read ALP or ALPACCA as nickel silver that may or may not be plated.

How it's marked

  • Named outright, as on the teaspoon and the trophy plate above: NICKEL SILVER, or a trade name such as ALP, beside the maker's name. Any of the names above may appear.
  • When plated: EPNS, electroplated nickel silver, sometimes with a grade or a pattern name. The silver is the thin coat; the body is nickel silver.
  • Never sterling or coin. US law forbids those words on plated wares, and a fineness number on silver states silver content; on nickel silver there is none to state.

German silver is not the same as silver made in Germany. German silver wares of solid silver are marked with a number such as 800 and, from 1888, a crescent moon and a crown: see what 800 means.

Questions

Does German silver have any silver in it?

No. German silver, also called nickel silver, is an alloy of copper, nickel and zinc. The name describes its color, not its metal.

Is German silver the same as nickel silver?

Yes. They are two names for the same family of copper, nickel and zinc alloys; nickel silver became the usual trade name in the US after the First World War.

What does EPNS mean?

Electroplated nickel silver: a nickel silver body with a thin coat of silver laid on by electroplating. The silver is only on the surface.

How do I tell German silver from sterling?

Read the marks first: sterling is marked STERLING, 925 or with a lion passant, and German silver is often marked with one of its names, such as NICKEL SILVER, or EPNS when plated. An unmarked item needs a test of the metal.
