Noritake marks

Noritake's backstamp changed with its history: Nippon before 1921, Made in Japan after, a different name just after the war, and a new letter in the wreath in the 1950s.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

Noritake's factory company, Nippon Toki, was founded in 1904 near Nagoya by the Morimura trading group 1, 2, 4. An M (for Morimura) in a wreath with Nippon points to before 1921, and with Made in Japan to 1921 or later 4, 5, 6; Rose China points to about 1946 to 1948 4, 6, 11; an N in a wreath to 1953 or later 4, 9, 10.

Who made Noritake

In 1876 Ichizaemon Morimura and his brother Toyo founded the trading firm Morimura Gumi in Tokyo, and Toyo opened a store in New York that became Morimura Brothers, an importer of Japanese goods 1, 2, 9. Porcelain became a growing part of its trade 1, 2.

In 1904 the group founded Nippon Toki Gomei Kaisha, with its factory at Noritake, a village near Nagoya; the place name became the brand 1, 2, 4. In 1914 it finished Japan's first Western-style dinner set, the Sedan pattern, and sent it to the United States 1, 2, 4. The company was reorganized as Nippon Toki Kabushiki Kaisha in 1917 1, 2, 4.

Morimura Bros. closed its New York office in December 1941 2, 9. Export trade reopened in August 1947 1, 4. In 1981 the company took the name Noritake Co., Limited 1, 18, which still makes tableware.

Noritake marks, in order

Where we have no open-license photograph or period-book facsimile of a mark, its card describes it in words. Noritake used hundreds of backstamp variants; these are the main kinds and the dates they point to 4, 6.

Nippon-era marks

Before 1921

Before 1921 the Morimura group marked wares for the US with Nippon and one of its symbols: a maple leaf, an M in a wreath, the letters RC on a balancing-toy device, or a round komaru emblem 4, 5, 7, 8. Our Nippon marks page covers them.

Noritake with the M in a wreath

About 1912 or 1918 to about 1953

An M, for Morimura, in a leafy wreath open at the top, with Noritake above and Hand Painted, then Nippon or Made in Japan, below, printed usually in green, also red or blue 4, 5, 16. With Nippon it is before 1921; with Japan or Made in Japan, 1921 or later 4, 5, 6. Alden and Richardson date its first use with Noritake to 1918; Van Patten records a version registered in Japan in 1912 4, 5.

The komaru with Made in Japan

After 1921

The round komaru emblem with Made in Japan or Nippon Toki Kaisha, used especially on wares for Britain and Europe 6, 7.

Rose China

About 1946 to 1948

Just after the war, while its china was below the old standard, the company marked it Rose China rather than Noritake 4, 6, 11. Some versions have the initials RC with the komaru 4, 12.

Noritake with Made in Occupied Japan

August 1947 to April 1952

Noritake's backstamp of the occupation years adds Made in Occupied Japan 4, 9. Noritake told Van Patten it was not used before August 1947, when exports reopened 4. The occupation ended on April 28, 1952 12, 17. Wares of these years were also lawfully marked just Japan or Made in Japan, so the absence of Occupied does not rule them out 9, 13.

Noritake China with the M in a wreath, again

From 1948

From 1948 the Noritake name returned to the backstamp with the M in a wreath 4, 6.

The N in a wreath

From 1953

An N, for Noritake, in a wreath, with Noritake above and often China or Japan below; later versions add a bow, Bone China or the registered sign 4, 9, 10. It replaced the M in 1953, by most accounts; Van Patten says it was registered in Japan in 1953 but used from 1952 4, 9, 10.

Noritake with another country

From 1972

Noritake's own history lists a plant in Sri Lanka from 1972 and later ones in Indonesia and Thailand 1. A Noritake mark that names a country other than Japan points to one of these later plants.

Copied marks and look-alikes to be careful with

  • Copies of the M in a wreath appear on new porcelain made in the Nippon style: a wreath upside down with an hourglass inside, a wreath with a K inside, or a wreath closed at the top 4, 15. The genuine wreath is open at the top.
  • Noritake's own Australian company says its backstamps have been copied or put on items it did not make, and that an item's quality and style should match the era its mark claims 3, 4.
  • Made in Occupied Japan is not a Noritake mark. Many Japanese makers used it between 1945 and 1952 12, 13.
  • RC appears in two eras: on the Nippon-era Royal Crockery mark and on the postwar Rose China mark. The letters alone do not date an item 4, 10, 12.

Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine. A mark says what it claims; a specialist who can handle the item is the one to ask.

Where the sources disagree

  • The New York store: Noritake's history has Toyo Morimura open Morimura Brothers in 1876 1; Morimura Bros.' own timeline has a store called Hinode Shokai in 1876, renamed Morimura Brothers in 1881 2.
  • The first Noritake M in a wreath: 1918 according to Alden and Richardson 5, or registered in Japan in 1912 according to Van Patten 4.
  • Rose China: 1946 to 1947 in some books 4, 6, 11, 1946 to 1948 in another 14.
  • The N in a wreath: from 1953 3, 9, 10, or from 1952 as Van Patten records 4.

Questions

How old is my Noritake if it says Nippon?

Before 1921. After the US ruling of 1921 the same marks read Japan or Made in Japan 4, 5, 6.

What do the M and the N in the wreath mean?

M is for Morimura, the trading family behind the company, and was used until the early 1950s; N is for Noritake, from 1953 4, 5, 9.

What is Rose China?

The name Noritake put on its first postwar china, about 1946 to 1948, while it kept its own name off ware below its old standard 4, 6, 11.

Does Made in Occupied Japan mean Noritake?

No. Many makers used it between 1945 and 1952; Noritake's own backstamp of those years also carries the Noritake name 4, 9, 13.

When was Noritake founded?

Its factory company was founded in 1904 near Nagoya by the Morimura group, which dates from 1876 1, 2, 4.
