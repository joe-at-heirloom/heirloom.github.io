Russian silver marks: 84, 88 and the kokoshnik

Russian silver gives its fineness in zolotniks and, before 1896, its year. Here is how to read the numbers and marks, with the sources for each.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

84 on Russian silver means 84 zolotniks out of 96: 875 thousandths. 88 is 916 and 91 is 947. Before 1896 the assay master's mark carries the year; from 1896 to 1927 the state mark is a woman's head in a kokoshnik, facing left until 1908 and right after, with a Greek letter for the district. Soviet marks from 1927 are in thousandths.

The numbers: zolotniks, not thousandths

Before 1927, Russian fineness was counted in zolotniks: the parts of pure metal in a pound of 96 zolotniks. 96 would be pure. A law of 1847 fixed silver at three standards, kept by the assay charter of 1896:

MarkThousandthsNotes
84875The commonest Russian silver standard
88916 (917 rounded)A higher standard, often on Fabergé silver
91947The highest of the three

Gold used other numbers: 56 zolotniks is about 14 carat, 72 about 18 carat. Read 72 on a gold item as gold; it was a silver standard only in the 18th century.

Before the kokoshnik: town, year and assay master

Russian marking of gold and silver goes back to a decree of Peter the Great in 1700. Through the 18th and 19th centuries an item usually carried the arms of its town (Moscow: Saint George and the dragon; St Petersburg: crossed anchors with a scepter), the initials of the assay master with the year, the standard in zolotniks, and the maker's mark. From the 1770s to 1896 the year on the assay master's mark was compulsory, which makes these marks unusually easy to date.

Town marks before 1896

Moscow and St Petersburg assayed most Russian silver, but provincial towns struck their own arms as a town mark. These are the marks Marc Rosenberg printed in 1928. Most are confirmed by Postnikova-Loseva's standard Russian reference (1983), whose years, by assay master, are often wider or earlier than Rosenberg's; each card gives the years the sources agree on, and where they differ, the database keeps both. Vitebsk and Kamianets-Podilskyi rest on Rosenberg alone, and Postnikova-Loseva gives the gateway mark to Zhitomir.

The kokoshnik, 1896 to 1927

A new assay charter of March 11, 1896 brought one state mark for the whole empire: a woman's head in a kokoshnik, the tall traditional headdress, with the standard number beside it. Assay masters' marks no longer carry a year. Sources date the new mark from 1896 (the charter) or from 1899 (when it came into general use); read it as 1896 to 1899 onward.

  • Head facing left: the first type, with the Cyrillic initials of the district's assay chief. Up to 1908.
  • Head facing right: from 1908, with a Greek letter for the assay district in place of the initials: delta (Δ) for Moscow. Museums date this type 1908 to 1917; Russia's assay chamber shows it in use to 1926, until the Soviet change of 1927.

The direction is a guide, not a rule: one Hillwood record notes a left-facing head on an item dated after 1908. The left-facing kokoshnik below is on a Fabergé fork in the Klingenmuseum Solingen; the other kokoshnik and Soviet marks are the printed drawings in Postnikova-Loseva (1983), official state signs.

Soviet marks, from 1927

In 1927 fineness moved to thousandths (875, 916, 925 on silver), and the state mark became a worker's head with a hammer. From 1958 it was a hammer and sickle inside a five-pointed star, with the fineness and a letter for the assay office, until 1994, when the kokoshnik returned with metric numbers. These dates come from Russia's Federal Assay Chamber; no second published source was found for them. The star with hammer and sickle appears on the gold marks page, beside 583.

Read these before you rely on a mark

  • The kokoshnik's start date (1896 or 1899) and the end of the right-facing type (1917 or 1926–27) differ between sources; both readings are given above.
  • Alpha for St Petersburg is given by one source only, so it is left out above; delta for Moscow is confirmed by two.
  • Russian marks, Fabergé's above all, are among the most copied in silver. A mark gives the date and standard it claims; whether the item is what it says is a question for a specialist.

Questions

What does 84 mean on Russian silver?

84 zolotniks out of 96: 875 thousandths, or 87.5% silver. It was the commonest Russian silver standard from 1847 to 1927. 88 is 916 and 91 is 947.

What is the kokoshnik mark?

The Russian state mark from 1896 to 1927: a woman's head in a kokoshnik headdress, with the standard in zolotniks. Facing left it is usually before 1908; facing right, 1908 or later, with a Greek letter for the assay district.

How do I date Russian silver before 1896?

Look for the assay master's mark: until 1896 it carried the year in figures, often with the master's initials, beside the town arms and the standard number.
