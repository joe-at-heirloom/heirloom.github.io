Birmingham hallmark date letters, 1773 to 2000

Every date letter the Birmingham assay office used from 1773 to 2000, from the same table Heirloom Advisor dates silver with. Type a letter to see every year it could be.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A Birmingham hallmark has an anchor as its town mark. To date it, match the date letter's case and style to one of the cycles below; a single letter appears in several cycles, so all three have to agree. Until 1974 each letter covers an assay year, not a calendar year.

Find a letter

Type the letter you see. Every Birmingham year that used it appears here, with its style, so you can match the shape.

The other marks beside the letter

Birmingham's marks, and what they narrow

  • 1697–1720: From 27 Mar 1697 the Britannia standard (95.8% silver) was compulsory. Its marks are the figure of Britannia and a lion's head erased, in place of the lion passant and leopard's head. Sterling was allowed again from 1 Jun 1720, and Britannia stayed optional afterwards. From 1697 to 1701 only London could assay; Chester used Britannia marks 1701-1719/20. Britannia plus lion's head erased with a London letter means 1697-1720 (or a later optional Britannia item). A sterling lion with a London letter from the 1697-1715 court-hand cycle is not expected.
  • 1784–1890: A sovereign's-head mark showed that plate duty had been paid. London struck it from 1 Dec 1784 (letter 'i' of 1784-85). At first the head was incuse (sunk), on letters i 1784-85 and k 1785-86, and it faced the other way from 1786. The duty was abolished in 1890: London dropped the head from 2 May 1890 (during 'O' 1889-90), and Birmingham from 2 May 1890 (during 'p'). A head present means 1784-1890. It is absent before Dec 1784 and after May 1890. Whose head it is (George III, George IV, William IV, Victoria) narrows further, but new heads were often introduced some years after a new reign.
  • 1798–1890: Watch cases were exempt from duty from March 1798, so watch cases hallmarked after the 1797-98 assay year have no sovereign's head. Do not use a missing duty head to date a watch case to before 1784 or after 1890.
  • 1773–1962: Birmingham's lion was passant guardant until 1875 and passant afterwards. Chester's was passant guardant until 1822, passant 1823-1883, then guardant again from 1884 until 1962. Separates Chester 1823-1883 from other Chester cycles, and Birmingham before and after 1875.
  • 1697–2000: London: leopard's head (crowned to 1821, uncrowned after; replaced by a lion's head erased on Britannia-standard plate). Birmingham: anchor, from 31 Aug 1773, unchanged. Sheffield: crown for silver from 1773; a rose from 1975, per silvermakersmarks and the SilverMine chart, which shows the rose with the 1975 'A'. Wikipedia says the rose came in 1977 (conflict). Chester: the city arms. Chaffers says the arms changed about 1720 to three demi-lions dimidiated with three half-garbs. Later Chester marks (e.g. 1938) show a sword between three wheatsheaves. Chester also struck a leopard's head with its marks until 1838-39: Jackson Tables II-VII include it, and Table VIII from 1839-40 does not. Identifies the office. A Chester mark with a leopard's head is 1838-39 or earlier, which separates Chester script 1726-50 from script 1901-25 (silvermakersmarks makes the same point).
  • 1660–1974: London changed its letter on 19 May (St Dunstan's day) until 1660 and on about 29 May from the early 1700s to 1974. Birmingham changed in July. Sheffield changed in July (the first Monday in July per silvermakersmarks). Chester changed about 5 July until 1839, 5 August 1839-1889, and 1 July from 1890. All offices change on 1 January from 1975. Turns a letter into calendar months (see year_label).
  • 1975 on: The Hallmarking Act 1973 came into force on 2 Jan 1975. From then all remaining offices use the same date letter, changing each 1 January: 1975 is script capital A and J is not used. The old cycles were cut short: London italic a-t ended at t (1974) and Sheffield italic A-G ended at G (1974). The Act also introduced platinum marks. A letter from 1975 on dates the item to one calendar year, whichever office struck it.
  • 1999 on: From 1 Jan 1999 a numeric fineness mark (e.g. 925) is compulsory. The date letter and the traditional lion passant became optional, so a modern item may have no date letter. A 925 mark with no date letter is consistent with 1999 or later.
  • 1934–2023: Optional commemorative marks: George V Silver Jubilee (twin heads), 1934-35, with London letters t and u (Birmingham from Feb 1934). Elizabeth II Coronation, 1953-54, with London R (1952-53) and S (1953-54). Silver Jubilee 1977 (C). Millennium mark 1999 and 2000. Also Golden Jubilee 2002, Diamond Jubilee 2012, Platinum Jubilee 2022 and the Charles III Coronation 2023. A commemorative mark pins the year to those listed.
  • 1867 on: From 1867 imported plate was additionally struck with an 'F' in an oval. From 1904 imported wares received separate import assay-office marks instead of the usual town marks. An 'F' in an oval means imported, 1867 or later. A foreign-ware office mark means imported, 1904 or later: do not read it as British-made.
  • 1701–1962: Birmingham and Sheffield offices were founded by the Act of 1773 (Birmingham opened 31 Aug 1773; Sheffield's first item was 20 Sep 1773). Chester was re-established as a statutory office in 1701 and closed on 24 Aug 1962. No Birmingham or Sheffield letters before 1773; no Chester letters after 1962.

Every cycle

Letters to 1923 are facsimiles of the punches, printed in W. Chaffers, Handbook to Hall Marks on Gold and Silver Plate (1924), public domain, via the Internet Archive. Later letters, and the few the book doesn't show clearly, are set in a similar typeface. Where an openly licensed photograph of a later cycle exists, it is shown under that cycle's heading. A flagged year is one where the sources disagree.

Read these before you rely on a year

  • Until 1974 a date letter covers an assay year, not a calendar year. 'year' is the first calendar year of that assay year and 'year_label' gives the span (London 's' of 1753 = 29/30 May 1753 to May 1754). The letter changed about 29 May in London and in July at Birmingham, Sheffield and Chester (5 August at Chester 1839-1889). From 1975 it changes on 1 January everywhere.
  • The same letter in the same case recurs in several cycles of one office. For example, London Roman lower case was used in 1736-55, 1776-95, 1816-35 and 1896-1915. So case, style, shield shape and the other marks (see rules) must all agree before a row is used. Shield shapes are not in this table.
  • 'style' uses short descriptors: gothic = black letter / Old English; court_hand; roman; italic; script; block = plain sans-serif capitals (Sheffield 1868-92). style_basis 'label' means Chaffers or Jackson names the style in the cycle heading. 'glyph' means no textual label was found, so the style was read from the letterforms in silvermakersmarks and the SilverMine charts, which agree. This applies to all post-1924 cycles and some Chester cycles.
  • 1975-1999 and 2000 rows are repeated for London, Birmingham and Sheffield because the letter was shared by all offices (Edinburgh too). In 1975-1999 the ninth letter, 1983, is a script J in Birmingham Assay Office's own archive (earlier sources read it as I). 2001-2024 (lower case, per silvermakersmarks with i omitted so 2008 = j) and the 2025 cycle (from 'A') are left out: only one reliable source gives the letters year by year.
  • Not covered: Edinburgh, Glasgow and Dublin (skipped rather than guessed; their cycles have irregular years and duplicate letters that could not be cross-checked in the time available), and the closed offices of Exeter, Newcastle, York and Norwich. Birmingham and Sheffield opened in 1773; Chester's table starts with the 1701 statutory cycle and ends when the office closed on 24 Aug 1962.

Questions

How do I find the year from a Birmingham hallmark?

Check the town mark first: Birmingham used an anchor. Then find the date letter, note whether it is a capital or a small letter and its style, and match all three to one cycle above. A letter usually covers an assay year that straddles two calendar years, so most entries read like 1753-54.

Why does the same letter appear in more than one cycle?

Each office ran through the alphabet again and again, changing the letter style and usually the shield shape each time. The same small Roman letter can mean four different years, so the style, the case and the other marks have to agree before a year is certain.

What if my letter doesn't match any style exactly?

Then the year is uncertain, and the honest answer is a range. Worn punches make letters look alike: a long s can read as f, I or l. Heirloom Advisor reports every year a letter could be rather than picking the nearest one.
