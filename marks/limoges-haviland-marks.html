Limoges and Haviland marks

Limoges is a town in France, not a single maker. A Limoges plate often carries two marks from two different firms, and knowing which is which is most of the work.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

Dozens of factories made porcelain at Limoges 1, 2, 3. A mark printed in green under the glaze usually names the factory that made the white porcelain; a mark in red, brown, gold or another color over the glaze names whoever decorated it, which can be another firm or an American studio 1, 8. France in the mark usually means 1891 or later, when the US began requiring the country of origin 1, 4.

Who made Limoges porcelain

Limoges, in the Haute-Vienne, became a porcelain town after kaolin was found nearby at Saint-Yrieix in 1768 1, 2. By the late 1800s it had many factories, among them Alluaud, Pouyat, Tressemanes & Vogt, Délinières, Guérin, Lanternier, Bernardaud, Gérard Dufraisseix and the Havilands 1, 2, 3. A US customs decision of 1907 counted some thirty-six makers in and around the town and forty or more firms that decorated china 5.

Much of it was sold white, as blanks, to decorators: in Paris, and later in America, where decorating studios and amateur china painters added their own decoration and marks 1, 8.

Haviland & Co. was founded by David Haviland, a New York china importer, who settled in Limoges in the early 1840s to make porcelain for the American market 1, 6. In the 1870s and 1880s the firm also ran an art-pottery studio at Auteuil, near Paris, making painted earthenware and stoneware 1, 7.

His son Theodore Haviland left the family firm and set up his own company and factory in Limoges around 1890 to 1893; the sources give different years 1, 6. From then on there were two separate Haviland firms with separate marks 1, 3. A third, Charles Field Haviland, ran a different Limoges factory whose marks read CFH 1, 3.

Limoges and Haviland marks, in order

Blank marks, under the glaze

The 1800s on

The maker's initials or name, often with Limoges and later France, printed under the glaze, usually in green, when the white porcelain was fired. It names who made the porcelain, not who painted it 1, 8. Jervis printed separate white-ware and decorated-ware marks for several Limoges firms in 1897 1.

Decorating marks, over the glaze

The 1800s on

A name or initials, often in a circle or in script, fired on over the glaze with the decoration, in red, brown, gold or another color 1, 7. It names who decorated or sold the item: the factory itself, another firm, an American studio, or an amateur. A painted name and date, such as the “Lottie 2-13-1893” on a Haviland dish at the Cooper Hewitt, are the painter's, not the factory's 1, 8.

France in the mark

Usually 1891 or later

The McKinley Tariff required imports to the US to be marked with their country of origin in English from March 1, 1891 1, 4. Jervis notes that one Limoges firm added France to its mark in 1891 1. So France usually means 1891 or later; a mark without it can be earlier, or made for a market outside the US 1, 4. It is a rule of thumb, not proof.

H & Co impressed, on Haviland stoneware

About 1873 to 1885

H & Co impressed in the clay, sometimes in a beaded circle, is found on the earthenware and stoneware of Haviland's Auteuil studio, which the Met dates about 1880 to 1885 1, 7. It is Haviland art pottery, not Limoges porcelain.

H & Co over L, in green

1876 to about 1891

H & Co over a line with L below, under the glaze in green: Haviland & Co.'s mark on its own white porcelain 1, 7. The Haviland books date the green H & Co marks from 1876 10, 11, 12, and without France it most likely predates the 1891 rule; museums date such items from about 1876 to 1890 7, 9.

H & Co, L and France

From 1891

The same blank mark with France added, from 1891 1, 4. The Cooper Hewitt records a dish with this mark that was decorated in America and signed and dated 1893 by its painter 8. Jervis printed this mark as Haviland's white-china mark in 1897 1.

Haviland, France, in green

About 1893 to 1931

Haviland in script over France, under the glaze in green: Haviland & Co.'s later blank mark 3, 10, 12. Travis dates it 1894 to 1931; Gaston 1893 to 1930 in one edition and 1894 to 1931 in another 10, 12. Haviland & Co. was liquidated in 1931 10, 12.

Haviland & Co. Limoges, the decorating mark

From about the 1870s

The firm's full name over or around the word Limoges, fired on over the glaze, means Haviland decorated the item itself 1, 7. The colors and extra wording of these marks changed over the years; the sources used here do not date each version.

Theo Haviland, Limoges, France

From about 1892

Theodore Haviland's own firm marked its ware with his name, often written Theo Haviland, over Limoges and France 1, 3. It was a separate company from Haviland & Co. until 1941, when his son William bought back the old firm's names and designs 1, 3, 10, 11.

CFH and GDM

1868 to the 1890s

CFH is Charles Field Haviland's factory, the old Alluaud works; GDM was added under its successors Gérard, Dufraisseix and Morel from about 1881, and France from 1891 1, 3. These marks are not Haviland & Co.'s 1, 3.

Other Limoges makers' marks

Jervis printed these marks of other Limoges firms in 1897; Cushion lists the same makers 1, 3.

Look-alike and later marks to be careful with

  • Two marks, two firms. Most of the questions about Limoges come from reading the decorator's mark as the maker's, or the reverse 1, 8.
  • Three Havilands. Haviland & Co. (H & Co), Theodore Haviland (Theo Haviland) and Charles Field Haviland (CFH) were separate businesses with separate marks 1, 3.
  • Haviland made in America. From 1936 to about 1957 Shenango China of New Castle, Pennsylvania, made dinnerware for Theodore Haviland, marked Theodore Haviland New York and Made in America 10, 13, 15; the trademark office records the mark as registered in 1951, claiming use from 1938 14. That ware is American, not from Limoges.
  • Limoges made in Ohio. The Limoges China Company of Sebring, Ohio, put Limoges in its marks; threatened with a lawsuit from France, it changed to American Limoges, a mark it filed in 1941 13, 16.
  • Since 2017 the name Porcelaine de Limoges has been protected in France for porcelain made entirely in the Haute-Vienne, partly against copies, Euronews reported 17.

Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine. A mark says what it claims; a specialist who can handle the item is the one to ask.

Where the sources disagree

  • When Theodore Haviland set up on his own: after a partnership ended on January 1, 1890, in Jervis's 1897 book, but on January 1, 1892, in his 1902 encyclopedia 1; the late 1880s in Laurens d'Albis's account 6.
  • Haviland, France: 1894 to 1931 in Travis 10, 1893 to 1930 in Gaston's first edition 12.
  • The American-made Theodore Haviland ware: 1936 to 1958 in Lehner 13, to 1957 in Travis 10.
  • When Sebring's Limoges China Company became American Limoges: from 1941, by its trademark filing, in Lehner 13; by 1946, in the Sebring Ohio Historical Society's account 16.

Questions

Why does my Limoges have two marks?

One is usually the factory's mark, printed under the glaze in green when the white porcelain was made; the other is the decorator's, fired on over the glaze with the painting 1, 8. They can be two different firms.

Is Limoges a brand?

No. It is a town in France with many porcelain factories, and Limoges in a mark tells you where, not who 1, 2, 3.

What does France on a Limoges mark tell me?

That it was most likely marked in 1891 or later, when the US began requiring imports to name their country of origin 1, 4.

Are Haviland and Theodore Haviland the same company?

No. Theodore Haviland left Haviland & Co. and founded his own firm around 1890 to 1893, with his own marks 1, 3, 6.

My plate has a name and a date painted on the back. Is that the factory?

Usually not. Painted names and dates are often the painter's, for example an amateur who decorated a Limoges blank 1, 8.
