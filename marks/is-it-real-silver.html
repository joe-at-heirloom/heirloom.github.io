Silver or silver plate? What the marks say

Most of the answer is in the small stamps. Here is which marks say solid silver, which say plate, and what no mark can tell you.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A mark that states a standard means solid silver at that standard: STERLING or 925, COIN or 900, a number such as 800, or a British hallmark with a lion passant. EP, EPNS, silver plate, nickel silver and German silver mean plate or no silver at all. A maker's name on its own says nothing about the metal, and no mark is proof: only a test of the metal settles it.

Heirloom doesn't decide whether an item is genuine. What a mark can do is say which kind of metal the maker or an assay office put on record, and that's usually the first question about a spoon, a tray or a candlestick that looks like silver: is it solid silver, or silver plate?

Marks that say solid silver

These marks state a standard: how many parts in 1000 of the metal are silver.

The markWhat it statesWhat it means
STERLING, STER. or 925Silver of at least 925 parts in 1000Solid silver. In the US the word may be used only at 925 or better, and never on plate.
COIN or COIN SILVERAt least 900 in 1000Solid silver, the American coin standard. See coin silver.
A number: 800, 900, 925, 958, 999That many parts of silver in 1000Solid silver at that standard. See what the numbers mean.
A British hallmark with a lion passantSterling, 925Solid silver, tested and marked by an assay office. A figure of Britannia instead means the higher Britannia standard, 958.
A crescent moon and a crown, with a numberGerman silver wares from 1888 at the fineness shownSolid silver, 800 or better.

In Britain those marks are struck by an assay office after it has tested the metal. In the United States the maker stamps them, and the law holds the maker to the standard the mark states: 925 for sterling and 900 for coin, within four parts in 1000.

Marks that say plate, or no silver at all

The markWhat it isWhat it means
EP, EPNS, silver plate, silver platedA coat of silver over another metalPlate. US law forbids STERLING or COIN on plated wares.
NICKEL SILVER, GERMAN SILVER, ALPACCA, ARGENTANAn alloy of copper, nickel and zincNo silver in the metal itself. See German silver.
Britannia metal (not Britannia silver)A tin alloyNo silver: a pewter-like metal, often plated.
A maker's name and a pattern name onlyNothing about the metalEither. Read the other marks, and see below.

Plated wares often carry a row of small marks that look like a hallmark at a glance: the maker's initials in shields, a pattern number, a registration mark. Look for a standard: a lion passant, Britannia, a crescent and crown, or a number such as 925. Without one, read it as plate until something else shows otherwise.

What marks can't tell you

  • No mark is not the same as no silver. Much early American silver carries only a maker's name, light items are exempt from hallmarking in Britain, and Germany's law of 1884 made a fineness mark optional.
  • A mark is a claim. Marks can be struck lightly, worn away, faked or set into a later item; Jackson and Chaffers both record frauds of this kind. A strong set of marks is good evidence, not proof.
  • Only a test of the metal settles it. An assay office, or a specialist with the right equipment, can test the metal. Heirloom reads marks; it doesn't test metal.

Questions

How can I tell if silver is real from the marks?

Look for a mark that states a standard: STERLING, 925, COIN, a number such as 800, or a British lion passant. Those mean solid silver at that standard. EP, EPNS, plate, nickel silver and German silver mean plate or no silver. A maker's name alone says nothing about the metal.

Does 925 mean real silver?

925 means the metal is stated to be 925 parts silver in 1000: sterling. In Britain an assay office strikes it after testing; in the United States the maker stamps it, and the law holds the maker to it. A stamp is still a claim, and only a test of the metal settles it.

Is silver plate worth keeping a record of?

Yes. A record is about what an item is and where it came from, not its price. Plated tableware often carries a maker's mark, a pattern and a family story worth writing down.

My silver has no marks. Is it plate?

Not necessarily. Much early American silver carries only the maker's name, and light items are exempt from hallmarking in Britain. Without a standard mark the metal is simply unknown until it is tested.
