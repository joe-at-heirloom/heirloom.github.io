1847 Rogers Bros. marks

The most common name on old American silverware. Here is who made it, what each part of the mark means, and why 1847 isn't the year your spoon was made.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

1847 Rogers Bros. is silver-plated flatware made by the Meriden Britannia Co. of Meriden, Connecticut, from about 1862, and by the International Silver Co. from 1898. 1847 is the year the Rogers brothers' business counted from, not the date of manufacture. Unless the mark also says STERLING, the item is plate; date it from its pattern.

Who made it

Asa H., Simeon S. and William Rogers were silversmiths in Hartford, Connecticut, who began making silver-plated spoons and forks there in the 1840s. The sources give different starting years: one 1901 county history gives 1844, 1846 and 1847 in three different family sketches, and a 1913 history says “about 1847”. The company that inherited their name settled on 1847 and put it in the mark.

In 1862 the brothers closed their Hartford works and the Meriden Britannia Co. of Meriden, Connecticut, took over their tools and dies, with the brothers supervising the work on a royalty. Meriden Britannia made 1847 Rogers Bros. flatware from then on, and in 1898 it was one of the founding companies of the International Silver Co., which kept the brand going well into the 20th century (it registered the name for stainless steel flatware in 1953). The company's own paper said in 1906 that the brothers had discovered electroplating in 1847; that is the maker's advertising, and no independent history says the same.

The marks are stamped on the back of the handle, near where it meets the bowl or tines. The trademark registration claims the 1847 ROGERS BROS. mark was first used in 1862, fifteen years after the date it carries.

Is it plate or sterling?

Almost always plate: the period catalogs and the 1896 trademark directory list 1847 Rogers Bros. as plated ware, silver over a nickel-silver base. From 1940 International Silver also sold sterling under the name, and those items say STERLING beside it.

Read the whole stamp first. American sterling is marked STERLING. Plate is marked with the maker's name and often a grade word or number, such as A1, XII, XIV, TRIPLE or QUADRUPLE PLATE, and a grade mark appears only on plate.

Then look at the places that wear. Plate is a thin coat of silver over a base metal, usually nickel silver, so on a well-used spoon the tip of the bowl, the back of the bowl and the ends of fork tines turn yellowish or gray where the silver has worn through to the metal underneath.

How to date 1847 Rogers Bros.

American plate makers didn't stamp a year. A number in the mark, such as 1847 or 1865, is the year a firm said it was founded and stayed on the ware for decades. To date an item, name its pattern (the design of the handle) and find when that pattern was introduced. A patent date stamped on a handle (PAT. followed by a date) is the earliest the design can be, not the year the item was made, and makers kept producing a popular pattern for decades.

  • Vintage, the grape-and-vine handle, is US design patent D37,059 of August 2, 1904, granted to Samuel Stohr for International Silver. The maker advertised it as new and patented in 1906.
  • Charter Oak was introduced in 1907 according to the maker's own paper of that year, which calls it the pattern for the business's 60th anniversary. Collectors' lists give 1906.
  • Before 1890: a jeweler's catalog of 1889 already sold 1847 Rogers Bros. flatware in the Arcadian, Assyrian, Crown, Lorne, Newport, Laurel, Embossed, Florentine, Daisy and Fairie patterns, so an item in one of those was made in 1889 or earlier, or is a later reissue.

Introduction years for the later patterns (First Love, Adoration, Eternally Yours, Remembrance, Heritage and others) are given on collectors' sites, but we haven't found two independent published sources for any of them, so they aren't listed here.

Not every Rogers is 1847 Rogers Bros.

The brothers licensed their name, and other firms used the surname on their own: Rogers & Bro. of Waterbury (a star before the name), C. Rogers & Bros. of Meriden (a different family), Wm. Rogers and Wm. A. Rogers. Courts stopped several imitators in the 1880s and 1890s. The Wm. Rogers page sets out which firm used which Rogers mark.

Questions

Does 1847 mean my spoon was made in 1847?

No. 1847 is the year the business counted as its founding, and it stayed in the mark for about a century. The mark itself was first used around 1862, and the maker said in 1906 that the number was a trademark, not a date. Date the item from its pattern instead.

Is 1847 Rogers Bros. real silver?

It is silver plate: a coat of pure silver over a nickel-silver base. Only items stamped STERLING beside the name, made by International Silver from 1940, are solid silver.

What do A1, XII and TRIPLE mean?

They are grades of plate. A1 is standard first-quality plate, XII is plate with extra silver on the parts that wear, and TRIPLE is the heaviest regular grade.

Who made 1847 Rogers Bros. silverware?

The Meriden Britannia Co. of Meriden, Connecticut, from 1862, and the International Silver Co. from 1898. The Rogers brothers of Hartford started the business and supervised the work in Meriden for some years after 1862.

Is 1847 Rogers Bros. the same as Wm. Rogers or Wm. A. Rogers?

No. Wm. Rogers marks come from William Rogers's later Hartford firm and from a Wallingford licensee; Wm. A. Rogers was an unrelated New York firm. See the Wm. Rogers page.
