Kangxi mark 康熙

The Kangxi reign of the Qing dynasty ran 1662–1722 (some tables from 1661). Its mark reads 大清康熙年製. Here is how it was written on imperial wares, and how often it was copied.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

The Kangxi mark is relatively uncommon on early Kangxi porcelain: in 1677 (the 16th year of the reign) the district magistrate Zhang Qizhong (張齊仲) forbade the potters of Jingdezhen to write the emperor's reign name or the sages' words on porcelain, lest they be broken, and empty double rings or symbols took their place. Ming marks (Chenghua, Xuande, occasionally Jiajing) are common on Kangxi wares. Six-character regular-script marks become standard late in the reign. Enamelled palace wares (falangcai) carry 康熙御製 in a double square.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Freely used on 19th- and 20th-century wares; Hobson says the bulk of Kangxi-marked items are modern, and the Kangxi mark is almost invariable on late-19th-century blue-and-white imitations. Unframed four-character Kangxi marks point to the Guangxu period or later.1, 3, 5, 6, 7

The Kangxi mark

  • 大清康熙年製 · 6 characters, regular script. Standard: underglaze blue, usually in a double ring, in two or three columns; marks became precise and small late in the reign.
  • 康熙御製 · 4 characters, regular script. Made by imperial order: the mark of the palace-workshop enamelled wares (falangcai), in blue or pink enamel within a double square.
  • 康熙年製 · 4 characters, regular script. Unframed four-character Kangxi marks point to the Guangxu period or later.
  • 大清康熙年製 · 6 characters, seal script. Disputed: Bushell had seen it on genuine items but notes it is often counterfeited; Gotheborg has not confirmed it on genuine Kangxi items. Very rare at best.
2, 4, 5, 6, 8

Years: 1662–1722 (some tables from 1661). Era officially from 1662; the emperor succeeded in 1661, so Gulland, UCSD-style accession tables and Hobson's footnote give 1661.1, 2, 3, 9

Other spellings in older books and catalogs: K'ang-hsi, K'ang Hsi, Kang-he (Gulland), Khang-hi (Bushell, after French writers), Kang-hi.

On imperial wares

Later items with this mark

Freely used on 19th- and 20th-century wares; Hobson says the bulk of Kangxi-marked items are modern, and the Kangxi mark is almost invariable on late-19th-century blue-and-white imitations. Unframed four-character Kangxi marks point to the Guangxu period or later.1, 3, 5, 6, 7

A reign mark names the reign it claims. Heirloom never decides whether an item is of that period or genuine; a specialist who can handle it is the one to ask.
