Chinese reign marks

The six characters on the base of a Chinese porcelain bowl usually name a dynasty and a reign: 大清乾隆年製, “made in the Qianlong reign of the Great Qing.” Here is every reign mark from 1368 to 1916, how to read one, and why a mark is where dating starts, not where it ends.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A reign mark is read in columns from the right, top to bottom1, 2, 3. The first two characters name the dynasty, 大明 Ming or 大清 Qing; the next two the reign; the last two, 年製, mean “made in the period”1, 3, 4, 5. Marks of admired earlier reigns were put on later wares for centuries, so a mark names the reign it claims and does not by itself date the item1, 3, 4, 5, 8.

Find a reign mark

Look at the first column on the right. Pick the dynasty it names, or type a reign’s name.

How to read a reign mark

  • Read in vertical columns, top to bottom, with the columns taken from right to left.1, 2, 3
  • A six-character mark is usually written in two columns of three or three columns of two; occasionally in one line, vertical or horizontal (a horizontal line reads right to left).3, 4, 5
  • Six-character structure: characters 1-2 the dynasty (大明 Da Ming 'Great Ming' or 大清 Da Qing 'Great Qing'), 3-4 the era name, 5-6 年製 nian zhi 'made in the period'.1, 3, 4, 5
  • A four-character mark omits the dynasty and starts with the era name (e.g. 康熙年製).1, 3, 5
  • Marks come in regular script (楷書 kaishu) or seal script (篆書 zhuanshu); seal marks are commonly arranged as a square block, typically three columns of two characters for six characters, sometimes inside a square border.2, 3, 5, 6
  • Seal-script reign marks before the 18th century are very unusual and should be viewed with suspicion; seal marks became common from the Yongzheng reign on.1, 4, 7
  • Variants: 御製 yu zhi 'made by imperial order' in place of 年製 (recorded for Kangxi, Yongzheng, Qianlong, Jiaqing); 造 zao 'made' occasionally in place of 製.1, 4, 5
  • The mark is usually on the base, often in underglaze blue inside a double ring; also in iron-red, other enamels or gold over the glaze, incised, or impressed, and sometimes along the mouth rim or in a panel in the decoration.1, 3, 4

After the Qing: Hongxian and the Republic

ReignCharactersOlder spellingYearsMarks on imperial wares
Hongxian洪憲Hung-hsien19167, 21, 224-character regular script

After the Qing abdication (12 February 1912) there is no imperial reign mark. Republic-period (1912-1949) porcelain often carries apocryphal Qing marks, especially Qianlong, on imitations of imperial enamelled wares (also 古月軒 Guyuexuan marks), or hall marks such as 居仁堂製 Jurentang, named for the hall where Yuan Shikai held his enthronement audience in December 1915 (Maigoo). A Jurentang mark therefore points to the Republic period, not to an imperial reign.21, 22, 23, 24

A mark is not a date

  • A reign mark names the era it claims, not the date the item was made. Copied (apocryphal) marks of earlier reigns are extremely common, so a mark alone never dates a item; judge body, glaze, shape, decoration and the quality of the writing first.1, 3, 4, 5, 8
  • Copies were not always meant to deceive: Chinese potters put marks of admired earlier reigns on later wares out of reverence (in Chinese usage 寄托款 'homage mark' or 仿款 'imitation mark'). Auction catalogues call these 'apocryphal' marks.1, 3, 9
  • Even marks of the reign itself are not proof of imperial manufacture: private kilns (民窑 minyao) also wrote the current reign's mark, usually less carefully than on official wares (官窑 guanyao).3, 4, 7
  • Japanese potters also copied Chinese Ming marks, especially Jiajing and Wanli, on their own wares.4, 5
  • An empty double ring, or a symbol (leaf, lingzhi fungus, ruyi head) where a mark would be, is associated with the Kangxi reign after a local ban on writing the reign name; the practice was also copied later and empty rings occur in other periods, so it does not date a item either. Sources disagree on the year of the ban: 1667 or 1677 (see REPORT).1, 3, 4, 5, 7
  • Era-year ranges follow the Western-calendar convention most reference tables use. A new era name normally took effect on the lunar New Year after the previous emperor's death, so the last Chinese year of an era usually ends in January or February of the following Western year; that is the source of most one-year differences between tables.4, 5, 10, 11
  • A Hongxian (洪憲) mark is almost always later: the court porcelain made for Yuan Shikai in 1915–16 is marked 居仁堂製 (Jurentang) in red seal script, and 洪憲年製 items are mostly 1920s–30s private-kiln wares or modern copies (Baidu Baike 洪宪瓷, citing the Palace Museum Journal; Maigoo; the Met dates its Hongxian-marked vases 1930s–40s).
  • Marks of earlier reigns were already being copied at the imperial kiln in the 15th century: Chenghua-period strata at the Jingdezhen imperial kiln site held sherds marked 大明宣德年製 (excavated 1987; Baidu Baike 寄托款). In the Ming such marks are most common on Tianqi wares; under Kangxi, Xuande, Chenghua and Jiajing marks are common; from Guangxu to the Republic, Kangxi marks are the most copied (Baidu Baike; Hobson 1915).

Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine or of the period its mark names. A specialist who can handle it is the one to ask.

Reigns with few or no marks

Hongwu 洪武, 1368–1398

Official Hongwu porcelain is generally regarded as unmarked. Bushell (1899) knew only a few Hongwu-marked items 'of somewhat doubtful authenticity'; Hobson (1915) says few Hongwu-marked museum items even look Ming. A modern Shanghai Museum text quotes that Hongwu official wares carried no reign mark (洪武时不写年款).5, 12, 13 Hongwu-marked items exist but are later; Hobson describes a British Museum dish with the mark that is about a century later than it claims.12

Jianwen 建文, 1399–1402

No source consulted describes porcelain with a Jianwen mark. Treat any Jianwen-marked item as unverified.5, 8, 11, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18

Hongxi 洪熙, 1425

No Hongxi reign marks are recorded on porcelain; Bushell attributes the absence to the era lasting under a year.5

Zhengtong 正統, 1436–1449

Part of the mid-15th-century 'interregnum' or 'blank period' (空白期) of Zhengtong, Jingtai and Tianshun: official kiln wares of these reigns carry no reign marks. Records suggest reduced output rather than none, and excavations at the Jingdezhen imperial kiln site since 1988 have found official wares of the period, all unmarked.5, 12, 13, 19

Jingtai 景泰, 1450–1456

Part of the mid-15th-century 'interregnum' or 'blank period' (空白期) of Zhengtong, Jingtai and Tianshun: official kiln wares of these reigns carry no reign marks. Records suggest reduced output rather than none, and excavations at the Jingdezhen imperial kiln site since 1988 have found official wares of the period, all unmarked.5, 12, 13, 19

Tianshun 天順, 1457–1464

Part of the mid-15th-century 'interregnum' or 'blank period' (空白期) of Zhengtong, Jingtai and Tianshun: official kiln wares of these reigns carry no reign marks. Records suggest reduced output rather than none, and excavations at the Jingdezhen imperial kiln site since 1988 have found official wares of the period, all unmarked.5, 12, 13, 19

Taichang 泰昌, 1620

No Taichang marks recorded on porcelain ('apparently unrepresented', Hobson; 'conspicuous for their absence', Bushell).5, 12

Tianqi 天啟, 1621–1627

Tianqi-marked items are very rare and usually of inferior quality; official records are silent on the imperial kiln in the last Ming reigns. Bushell also notes small jars marked with the single character 天, said to abbreviate Tianqi.5, 12

Chongzhen 崇禎, 1628–1644

Very rare; Hobson knew only a pair of wine cups in the British Museum. Bushell records a dedication dated Chongzhen 9th year (1636) on a temple vase.5, 12, 20

Shunzhi 順治, 1644–1661

Authentic Shunzhi-marked porcelain is very rare ('virtually unknown', Hobson).5, 7, 12 Shunzhi seal marks are considered later inventions.1, 4, 5, 7, 10, 12, 16

Questions

How do I read the characters on the bottom of a Chinese vase?

Start at the top of the right-hand column and read down, then move one column to the left. A six-character reign mark reads dynasty, reign, then 年製 “made in the period”: 大明宣德年製 is “made in the Xuande reign of the Great Ming.” A four-character mark leaves out the dynasty.

Does a Qianlong mark mean my item is from the Qianlong period?

No. Qianlong marks were put on a great many later items, especially in the Republic period (1912–1949) and since. A mark tells you the reign it claims; the body, glaze, painting and form decide the date, and only someone who can handle the item can judge those.

What is seal script?

An older, squarer style of the characters, used on seals. Seal-script reign marks are rare before the Yongzheng reign (1723–1735) and usual under Qianlong, Jiaqing and Daoguang; from Xianfeng (1851) the regular script returns.

Why does some Chinese porcelain have no mark, or an empty double ring?

Most porcelain made for everyday use was never marked. Under Kangxi a local ban on writing the reign name led potters to paint an empty double ring or a small symbol instead; such rings were also used later, so they do not date an item either.
