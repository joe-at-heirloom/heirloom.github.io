Coin silver: what it is and how it's marked

Before sterling, most American silver was made to the standard of the coin in people's pockets. Here is what that means and how to read the marks.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

Coin silver is American silver made to the standard of US silver coin: 892.4 parts in 1000 under the Coinage Act of 1792, and 900 from 1837. It is marked COIN, PURE COIN or STANDARD beside the maker's mark, or often with the maker's name alone. American makers moved to sterling (925) in the 1850s and 1860s.

Coin silver is American silver made to the standard of the country's silver coin, about 900 parts in 1000. It is solid silver, a little lower than sterling's 925, and it was the usual American standard until the 1850s and 1860s, when makers moved to sterling.

The coin standard

  • 1792 to 1837: the Coinage Act of 1792 set silver coin at 1485 parts fine to 179 parts alloy, which is 892.4 in 1000.
  • From 1837: an Act of that year changed it to 900 in 1000.

Coin silver followed the coin of its day, so silver marked COIN before 1837 may be at the lower standard. The name is about the standard: it doesn't mean the item was made from melted coins.

How coin silver is marked

  • COIN, stamped beside the maker's mark, saying the metal was of the same fineness as silver money.
  • PURE COIN, or PURE SILVER COIN, seen on New England silver.
  • STANDARD, found on silver made around Philadelphia.
  • Only a maker's name or initials, as on the spoon above. A separate mark for the metal was not the custom, and a maker's mark says nothing about the metal on its own.

These words were the maker's own choice, and makers used different terms for their alloys. Since the Stamping Act of 1906 the law has set a standard for the word: an item marked COIN or COIN SILVER must be at least 900 in 1000, within four parts.

From coin to sterling

American makers began stamping STERLING in the late 1850s, and by the 1860s most had moved to the sterling standard of 925. See what 925 and 900 mean.

What the marks can't tell you

  • A maker's name doesn't make silver coin silver, and plenty of American silver of the period carries no word about the metal. Only a test of the metal settles the standard.
  • A mark is a claim, about the maker and the metal alike. Heirloom never decides whether an item is genuine.

Questions

What is coin silver?

American silver made to the standard of US silver coin: 892.4 parts in 1000 before 1837 and 900 after. It is solid silver, slightly below sterling, and was usual until the 1850s and 1860s.

Is coin silver real silver?

Yes. It is solid silver at about 90%, a little less than sterling's 92.5%. It is not plate.

Was coin silver made from melted coins?

The name refers to the standard: the metal was of the same fineness as silver money. It does not mean an item was made from coins.

What is the difference between coin silver and sterling?

Sterling is 925 parts silver in 1000; coin silver is 900, or 892.4 before 1837. American makers moved from coin to sterling in the 1850s and 1860s.
