French silver marks: the Minerva head and the maker's lozenge

Since 1838 French silver has carried two marks: the state's Minerva head and the maker's lozenge. Here is how to read them, and the small marks that stand in for them.

By Heirloom · Updated November 22, 2026

In short

A helmeted head of Minerva is the French mark for solid silver, used since 1838: with a 1 it is the first standard (950, today 925), with a 2 the second (800). Small items carry a boar's head (Paris) or a crab (elsewhere) instead. Beside it is the maker's lozenge, a diamond with initials and a sign. A square maker's mark means silver plate.

The system of 1838

French marks rest on a law of 1797, and the set still in use was introduced on May 10, 1838. From then on one punch showed both the standard and the assay office: the head of Minerva for silver. All gold and silver already on sale was re-marked for free, so French silver with only older marks was, by law, unmarked after 1838.

A full set of French marks on silver is two: the state's Minerva head (or a small-item mark), and the maker's lozenge. There is no date letter, so French marks date an item only to a period.

The Minerva head

The goddess Minerva in a helmet, in profile, in a small frame with a figure: 1 for the first standard, 2 for the second. The figure does not tell Paris from the rest of France. Paris punches carry no office sign; punches from the provincial offices add a small sign, under the chin on the first standard and before the forehead on the second.

The first standard was 950 thousandths. Today's official table gives 925 for the Minerva with a 1, and French law has listed 925 and 800 as the silver standards since 1994. The year of the change from 950 to 925 is often given as 1973, but no law or museum record found here confirms it, so a first-standard Minerva means 950 or 925 depending on its date.

When an item is part gold and part silver, and the second metal is more than 3% of its weight, the Paris eagle's head and boar's head are struck together in one oval.

The boar's head and the crab

Small silver items, too small to take the Minerva head, were marked with a single small punch: a boar's head in Paris and a crab in the provincial offices. These marks promise only the second standard, 800. The sources agree on their start in 1838, but not on when they stopped: the often-quoted 1961 could not be confirmed, a Senate report of 1983 still names the crab, and today's table shows a Minerva without a frame for small silver. No legible open-license photo of either was found for this page.

The maker's lozenge

The maker's mark has been a lozenge, a diamond standing on its point, since 1798. It holds the maker's initials and a sign of the maker's own choosing, such as a star, a key or a caduceus, which no other maker in the same district may use. The punch is registered by striking it on a copper plate at the assay office. An importer uses an oval instead.

A perfectly square maker's mark means plated ware, not silver: the law required it of makers of plated goods, and a decree of 1860 extended it to electroplaters. Christofle's marks on silver plate are of this kind; see Christofle marks.

French gold, in brief

From 1838 to 1919 French gold carried the head of a Greek physician with a figure for its standard, and small items an eagle's head (Paris) or a horse's head (elsewhere). A decree of June 1919 made the eagle's head the mark for all French gold. An eagle's head with a 3 is 18 karat; with a 1, 22 karat. For what the numbers mean, see gold karat marks.

Read these before you rely on a mark

  • Minerva heads are small and often worn: the figure 1 or 2 and the frame's shape matter more than the face.
  • The end of the boar's head and crab, and the year the first standard moved from 950 to 925, are not settled by the sources found here.
  • A mark says what the item claims. Old French marks have been copied; a specialist can tell a struck mark from a cast or added one.

Questions

What does the Minerva head mean on French silver?

It is the French state mark for solid silver, used since 1838. With a 1 it is the first standard (950, now 925); with a 2, the second standard (800). Silver plate never carries it.

What does a boar's head mean on silver?

It is the Paris mark for small silver items, used from 1838 on things too small for the Minerva head. It means silver of at least 800 thousandths. Outside Paris the same job was done by a crab.

Is French silver with a square mark solid silver?

No. A square maker's mark is the French mark for plated goods, including electroplate from 1860. Solid silver has a diamond-shaped lozenge and a Minerva head or a small-item mark.

How do I date French silver?

Only roughly. French marks have no date letter. A Minerva head means 1838 or later; marks before it are older. The maker's lozenge can narrow the date once the maker is known, since makers' working years are recorded.
